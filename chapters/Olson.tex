\title{An overview of Nduga phonology}
\abstract{I present an overview of the phonology of Nduga, a Sara-Bongo-Bagirmi language in Central African Republic. Nduga's consonant system includes implosives, prenasalized plosives, prenasalized fricatives, and labial-velar plosives. It has two rhotics, /r ɽ/. Nduga has eight oral monophthongs, one oral diphthong /ᵘa/, and five nasal vowels. Vowel length is contrastive. Oral vowels exhibit vowel harmony between /e o/ and /ɛ ɔ/. Final vowels often reduce to schwa or elide completely. Nduga has three tone levels. All nine combinations of tones occur on bisyllabic nominals. Verb infinitives have lexical tone, with the patterns HH, HM, MM, ML, and LL occurring on bisyllabic verbs.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:olson:introduction}

In this chapter, I present an overview of the phonology of Nduga (pronounced [ndū.ɡā]), a speech variety found in the northcentral part of Central African Republic. It is found mostly in the Nana-Grébizi prefecture (Kaga Bandoro subprefecture), but it is also found on the eastern border of Ouham prefecture (Kabo subprefecture). More precisely, it is spoken in and around the villages of Ouandago, Konvi, and Bissekebou (aka Bissikibou) \citep[7]{moehama2021}.

\textit{Ethnologue} considers Nduga to be a dialect of the Luto language (ISO 639-3 code: [ndy]), which as a whole is spoken by about 19,000 people in Central African Republic and Chad \citep{eberhard2024}. \textit{Ethnologue} lists five dialects of the Luto language: Luto, Nduga, Ndokoa, Wada, and Konga. The first four of these likely correspond to what \citet{boyeldieuetal2006} refer to as Luto (lto), Nduga (ndg), Ndoka (ndk), and Wad (wad), respectively. That source provides extensive wordlist data for these four varieties.\footnote{The data from \citet{boyeldieuetal2006} can be downloaded from the following URL: \url{https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Appendix:Proto-Sara-Bongo-Bagirmi\_reconstructions}.} The Nduga data therein were collected by Pierre Nougayrol and include about 460 lexical items.

Nduga is currently classified as part of the Sara-Bongo-Bagirmi (SBB) group within the Central Sudanic family. \citet[343--344]{dimmendaal2019} consider SBB to be essentially the same as “West Central Sudanic.” More general information can be found in \textit{Ethnologue}\footnote{\url{https://www.ethnologue.com/language/ndy}}, as well as \textit{Glottolog}\footnote{\url{https://glottolog.org/resource/languoid/id/luto1241}} \citep{hammarstrom2024}.

Some previous research exists on the various varieties of the Luto language. Linguistic descriptions include \citet{moundo1975} (Luto variety), \citet{ndoko1991} (Luto variety), and \citet{mololi2011} (Ndokoa variety). In addition, there is a language survey \citep{moehama2021}, demographic information in \citet[75--77]{nougayrol1990}, lexical data in \citet{boyeldieuetal2006}, a comparative paper looking at the evolution of labial-velar consonants \citep{olson2024} (Luto and Nduga varieties), and brief mention in several other articles by Boyeldieu and Nougayrol, as well as \citet[17]{tucker1956} (Ndokoa dialect) and \citet[34]{bender1997}. To my knowledge, the current work is the first to focus on Nduga phonology.

My research was conducted in Bangui, Central African Republic, during three visits from 2012 to 2017, for a total of about four weeks. I worked with a team of three speakers, who are fluent in both the Luto and Nduga dialects. Unless otherwise noted, the data in the paper are from our consulting sessions. Part of the research was done in collaboration with Paul \& Jo Murrell and Brad \& Maria Festen.

We collected mostly lexical data, but also some paradigms and texts. The wordlist in the appendix is based on the 204-item wordlist tool in \citet{monino1988}.

I discuss consonants in \sectref{sec:olson:consonants}, vowels in \sectref{sec:olson:vowels}, tone in \sectref{sec:olson:tone}, syllable patterns in \sectref{sec:olson:syllable}, and word patterns in \sectref{sec:olson:word}. Additional discussion is provided in \sectref{sec:olson:discussion}, including addressing the question of how Nduga relates to the rest of SBB. Concluding remarks are given in \sectref{sec:olson:conclusion}.

Phonemic forms are enclosed by forward slashes, e.g. /ᵑg͡b/, and phonetic forms are enclosed by square brackets, e.g. [ŋɡb]. Superscripts and tie bars are used in phonemic forms in order to help distinguish them visually from phonetic forms, as well as to help show the unitary nature of the phonemic digraphs and trigraphs. The phonetic data are transcribed using the extant International Phonetic Alphabet \citep{ipa2006}.

Several phonemes are rare: /p, ᵐb, h/ do not occur in the wordlist, while /b, f, k͡p, ᵑg͡b/ occur only once or twice. These phonemes are also the rarest ones in Nougayrol’s data, with 10 or fewer occurrences of each phoneme. The implosives /ɓ, ɗ/ are more common than their plain counterparts /b, d/ in the wordlist.

\citet{boyeldieuetal2006} include a retroflex lateral [ɭ] in their transcriptions of the Luto varieties. I initially employed the same transcription. However, examination of the acoustic properties (cf. \figref{fig:olson:assegai}) showed that the sound exhibits a short closure period of about 30 ms, which is indicative of a tap or flap \citep[130]{catford1977} rather than a lateral. In addition, there is a significant reduction in intensity during closure. These properties lead me to consider the sound to be a retroflex flap [ɽ] and transcribe it accordingly.

    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/OlsonFig1.png}
    \caption{Waveform and spectrogram of the word [ŋɡbā.ɽā] ‘assegai’ (\# 162)}
    \label{fig:olson:assegai}
\end{figure}

In word-initial position, the retroflex flap [ɽ] is preceded by a short vowel-like segment, as shown in \figref{fig:olson:burn}. This prothetic segment is discussed by \citet[240--241]{ladefoged1996} for a post-alveolar flap in Walpiri. They state that the “segment assists in setting up the acoustic cues for the flap, which thus become comparable in both initial and intervocalic environments” (p. 240).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/OlsonFig2.png}
    \caption{Waveform and spectrogram of the word [ɽū.ɡū] ‘to burn (transitive)’ (\# 18), with prothetic vowel-like segment}
    \label{fig:olson:burn}
\end{figure}

A similar prothetic segment has been observed for the word-initial labiodental flap [ѵ] in Mono \citep[126]{olson2005}. It has also been attested for alveolar trills in several languages \citep{bolter2021} (cf. \citealt[219]{ladefoged1996} for Italian). However, the alveolar trill /r/ in Nduga is not accompanied by this prothetic segment.

The phoneme /j/ is nasalized before a nasal vowel, as shown in \REF{ex:olson:nasalization}:

\ea \label{ex:olson:nasalization}
Nasalization of /j/ \\
\phonr{/j/}{[j̃]}{Ṽ}
\z

The nasalized allophone [j̃] could potentially be interpreted instead as a phoneme /ɲ/ which causes a following vowel to become nasalized. \citet{boyeldieuetal2006} implicitly follow this analysis as they employ the symbol [ɲ] for the sound in their transcriptions of the Luto varieties.

However, I opt to interpret [j̃] as an allophone of /j/ for a several reasons. First, nasal vowel phonemes are independently motivated in Nduga (cf. \sectref{sec:olson:vowels}). Second, I did not find other cases of nasalization on vowels occurring adjacent to a nasal consonant. Third, the speakers’ intuitions were that [j] and [j̃] comprise a single phoneme. Fourth, the Nduga speakers noted that the tongue does not touch the roof of the mouth during the articulation of the sound. Phonetically, the sound is [j̃], not [ɲ].

This situation, in which a phoneme has a robust presence only in grammatical particles, is attested elsewhere. \citet{parker2009} mentions three languages in which a consonant phoneme only occurs in an affix, and never in a lexical root: Arammba [stk] (PNG), Awara [awx] (PNG), and Guambiano [gum] (Colombia), cf. \citet{quigley2003}, \citet{branks1973}.

I consider /ᵘa/ [u̯a] to be a diphthong for several reasons. First, there are no other syllable medial approximant-vowel phonetic sequences in Nduga. Second, the approximant portion [u̯] of the phoneme is phonetically short. Third, on the two words in the wordlist that have falling tones on the phoneme ([k-u̯a᷆] ‘to die’ \# 113, [ní.ŋɡá ku̯â] ‘snake’ \# 172), the tone occurs on the [a] portion of the phoneme.

The long vowels could be interpreted as either phonemic long vowels (Vː) or sequences of identical vowels (V₁V₁). I have chosen the first option, because there are no cases of dissimilar vowel sequences (i.e. V₁V₂) in Nduga, given that [u̯a] is considered to be a diphthong.

The long vowels often bear two distinct level tones (cf. \tabref{tab:olson:longvowels}). The tone patterns attested on long vowels are: HH HM HL MM ML LH LL. Since long vowels can bear two tones, I generally write them with two identical vowel symbols in order to allow marking the two tones in a clear manner.

Short vowels sometimes have contour tones, which I consider to be sequences of level tones. This is discussed further in  \sectref{sec:olson:tone}.

One advantage of my analysis is that it matches native speaker intuitions.

The V syllable pattern only occurs word-initially. The CVː and Cu̯a syllable patterns only occur word-finally. Many of the words that we originally thought had more than two syllables turned out to be multimorphemic. For example, [nzà.kú.zì] ‘bottom’ (\# 44) likely derives from [nzà] ‘foot’ (\# 136) + [kú.zì] ‘house’ (\# 104). \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:olson:discussion}

Nduga is well-situated within the SBB family \citetext{\citealp{boyeldieu2006}; \citealp{dimmendaal2019}}. It has prenasalized stops, labial-velar stops, and implosives, all of which are common in SBB. It has closed syllables that result from the erosion of a final vowel *CVCV > CVC. One unique aspect of its consonant inventory is the absence of /tʃ/ and /dʒ/.

Nduga shares some characteristics with Sara and others with Bongo-Bagirmi. Like Sara, it has contrastive nasal vowels and a three-tone system. Like Bongo-Bagirmi, on the other hand, it has vowel harmony and a more robust set of fricatives than is typical of Sara. This harmonizes with \citet[4]{boyeldieu2006}'s view that Nduga is closely related to Sara, but it forms a separate branch.

Most verbs in our data begin with [k]. The same verbs in the wordlist found in \citet{boyeldieuetal2006} are given as vowel initial, without the [k]. Some forms from the two sources are compared in \tabref{tab:olson:initialk}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:olson:conclusion}

This paper makes a significant contribution to our understanding of a speech variety that has received very little attention in the literature. I’ve provided information about Nduga consonants, vowels, tone, syllable patterns, and word patterns.

At the same time, it is a modest first step in the large task of describing a language. There are many possible topics for further research. The tonal system of Nduga warrants a more in-depth investigation, especially how tone is realized in the tense-aspect-mood system. A deeper examination of H-spreading could determine if the process extends to other environments. Examining in more detail the relationship between the extant Nduga forms and those of Proto-SBB would inform our understanding of the historical development of the language. The occurrence of vowel reduction/elision at the phrasal level suggests the possibility of metrical structure in the language. The H-spreading process suggests that tonal feet may be present. Finally, it would be fruitful to examine morpho-syntactically complex forms in more detail to uncover possible additional phonological alternations in the language.
